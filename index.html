                    Guide

                    Using LeafScan well

                    How to take a photo it can read, what its answers mean, and where to get a second opinion.

                    Taking a good photo

                    1. One leaf that fills most of the square, showing the side with the spots.
                    2. Hold the phone flat above the leaf and keep still. Tilted or blurry photos are often wrong.
                    3. Even light: shade or near a window, no harsh shadows or dark corners.
                    4. Plain background if you can, such as paper, a table or your palm.
                    5. If the result is not sure, add 2 or 3 leaves from the same plant. More leaves give a more reliable answer.

                    Reading the result

                    LeafScan was tested on 3,583 field photos from public datasets that it had never seen (not yet photos from Philippine farms). Each photo is checked three ways (your square, the square mirrored, and the whole photo) and the answers are combined. This is how often it was right at each level:

                    High confidence 70% and above
                    Right about 9 in 10 times. Still compare the signs before you act.
                    Medium confidence 45 to 70%
                    Right about 7 in 10 times. Answer the questions or add another leaf.
                    Not sure below 45%
                    Right only about 4 in 10 times. It does not mean the plant is healthy.
                    Not recognized below 30%
                    The photo may not be a leaf, or a plant LeafScan knows.

                    Tell LeafScan the plant if it guessed wrong. Use “Wrong plant?” on the result. On the same test photos, choosing the right plant raised accuracy from 77% to 91%.

                    Cucumber, squash and zucchini look alike. When LeafScan is not told the plant, it names the problem for the whole squash family (the same disease has the same cause and care on all three). On 301 test photos of these plants this raised right answers from 63% to 79%. Choose your plant to narrow it down.

                    Getting a second opinion

                    • Bureau of Plant Industry (BPI), Crop Pest Management Division, Quezon City: free diagnosis for farmers and students. Bring freshly collected sick plants (and whole insects) and fill in their diagnosis form; results that need a lab culture take about 1 to 6 days. BPI service guide
                    • UPLB Pest Clinic: send fresh sick leaves by courier, and they email a diagnosis report, usually within 7 working days. How to send a sample
                    • DA Regional Crop Protection Center at your Department of Agriculture regional office. Some run a Plant Health Clinic.
                    • An agriculture teacher, or the agriculture college of a state university near you.

                    Use “Show to a technician” on a result to send the leaf photos and LeafScan's answer together.

                    Philippine rules for treating plants

                    • Registered products only. Chemical pesticides must be registered with the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA); organic and biological products (botanicals, microbials) are registered with DA-BAFS (RA 11511). Check the active ingredient, that your crop is on the label, and that the registration has not expired. Some pesticides are banned or restricted.
                    • Neem and soap sprays are insect controls, not disease cures. In the FPA list (30 September 2026), neem (azadirachtin) is registered only as an insecticide for certain crops, and insecticidal soap only for Cavendish banana. LeafScan suggests them only against the insects that spread viruses.
                    • Do not burn sick plants in the open. The Ecological Solid Waste Management Act (RA 9003) bans open burning of farm waste. Uproot sick plants, and bury them or seal them in a bag away from the field.
                    • Homemade plant sprays are not proven against diseases. In a DOST-PCAARRD and UPLB project, some local plant extracts killed insect pests, but plain extracts did not stop plant pathogens in lab tests, and none stopped bacterial wilt.
                    • Some diseases are under quarantine. Panama disease (Fusarium wilt) of banana, coconut cadang-cadang, cassava witches' broom and citrus huanglongbing have BPI or PCA rules against moving planting material. Do not move plants, suckers, cuttings or soil from a sick farm; report it to the DA regional office, BPI or PCA.
                    • Spraying comes last. Philippine guides and the national IPM program (KASAKALIKASAN) put sanitation, crop rotation, resistant varieties and healthy seedlings first.

                      About LeafScan

                      A student research prototype: Developing a Prototype for Plant Disease Detection Using Image Recognition. A MobileNetV3 image recognition model runs entirely on your device with ONNX Runtime Web, so photos are never uploaded. Disease information is summarized from CABI, PlantVillage (Penn State) and university extension sources, with Philippine notes from PhilRice, DA regional offices, ATI, BPI, DOST-PCAARRD and UPLB, and scientific names checked against GBIF. Summaries have not yet been reviewed by a plant pathologist. Non-commercial use only.
